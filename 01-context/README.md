# 01 · Context: Ethiopia's Structural Problem

Ethiopia's development challenge is usually presented as a list of deficits. It is better understood
as a **system of mutually reinforcing constraints** in which each problem is simultaneously a cause
and a consequence of the others. This section establishes the diagnosis with evidence, then maps the
causal structure that any intervention must respect.

## The one-paragraph version

Ethiopia's economy is **agrarian but underproductive**: ~60% of employment and ~32% of GDP come from
agriculture, but >95% of output comes from 12+ million smallholders on farms under one hectare, with
cereal yields near 2.6 t/ha, ~5.7% mechanisation, 4–5% irrigation, and 15–25% post-harvest losses.
Because productivity is low and rain-fed, incomes are low and volatile; because incomes are low,
domestic demand is thin and savings cannot finance inputs; because finance is scarce (only ~2% of
ETB 2.58 trillion in annual agri-credit demand is met), farmers cannot buy the inputs that would
raise productivity. Meanwhile the state's ability to break the loop is constrained by a narrow tax
base, a debt-distress position, a foreign-exchange shortage, a state-dominated banking and telecom
sector, and recurrent conflict — which itself destroys the assets and market access the loop depends
on. Digital infrastructure matters here because it can lower the cost of **several links in the loop
at once** rather than one.

**Interpretation.** This is a *scale* problem disguised as a *technology* problem. Even a large
percentage improvement in yield on a 0.7-hectare farm produces a small absolute income gain, so
productivity interventions only pay off when combined with **market access** (to sell the surplus at
a fair price) and **aggregation** (to make inputs, mechanisation, credit and logistics affordable).
That combination is precisely what a platform can supply.

among the lowest shares of any major producer (Vietnam ~92%, Brazil ~90%, Kenya ~70%) — a direct
measure of how much value is lost between the farm gate and the port.

## The five structural constraints, stated precisely

### 1. Low factor productivity

Yields are far below potential, mechanisation and irrigation are marginal, and input use is low.
Fertiliser application at ~45 kg/ha is well below the ~200 kg/ha agronomic benchmark cited in
Ethiopian studies. Improved seed adoption is uneven. Soil degradation is significant. Extension,
though nominally large (50,000–60,000 development agents), is thinly spread.

### 2. Fragmented and thin markets

Domestic markets are physically and informationally fragmented. Prices for the same commodity can
differ sharply across markets in the same week. Only a small minority of farmers have access to
auction or exchange price information. Intermediary chains are long, and quality is inconsistently
graded. The **Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX)** was created in 2008 to address this and has
digitised (online trading launched January 2025; ~ETB 12.5 bn processed by mid-2025), but trading
volumes have at times fallen sharply and the exchange's reach to smallholders remains limited.

### 3. Logistics and infrastructure cost

Only **~13% of roads are paved** versus a Sub-Saharan average of ~25%; road density is ~148 km per
1,000 km² versus an African average of ~204. Traffic congestion alone is estimated to cost Ethiopia
~3% of GDP annually. Post-harvest losses of 15–25% for cereals and 30–50% for horticulture represent
food that was grown, harvested, and then thrown away — a direct, measurable, addressable inefficiency.

### 4. Financial exclusion and the agricultural credit gap

- **49%** of adults had a financial account in 2024 (up from 46% in 2021, 22% in 2014).
- **39.9 million adults** remain unbanked.
- Mobile money account ownership: **9.5%** (Findex) to **19.4%** (alternate series) — definitions differ.
- Only **21%** of adults made or received a digital payment.
- Formal institutions meet **~2%** of an estimated **ETB 2.58 trillion** annual agricultural credit demand.
- Only **8%** of commercial bank lending and **18%** of MFI lending goes to agriculture (2023/24).
- Of ETB 35 bn in agricultural credit disbursed in 2022, **almost two-thirds went unused** — a
  demand-side capacity problem, not only a supply-side one.

> This last point is strategically important: the constraint is not *only* money. It is the
> **absence of bankable, verifiable information about smallholder borrowers.** That is exactly what a
> transaction-generating platform produces.

### 5. Institutional and governance friction

- Corruption Perceptions Index: **38/100, rank 96/182 (2025)** — improving (34 → 37 → 38 across
  2021 → 2024 → 2025) but still below the global average of 42.
- State-owned enterprises and state banks dominate strategic sectors; state-owned credit share in
  total domestic credit rose from ~14% (2007) to ~60% (2018), crowding out private lending.
- Regulatory processes are slow and discretionary; the telecom and banking sectors have opened only
  recently (Safaricom licence 2021; mobile money for foreign-owned firms 2023).
- Public service delivery is uneven: the UHC service coverage index is **~39–46%** nationally, with
  regional scores ranging from ~23% (Somali) to ~64% (Addis Ababa).

## Human capital: the quiet multiplier

Two figures dominate any discussion of Ethiopia's human capital, and both are worse than the popular
narrative suggests:

- **90% of 10-year-olds cannot read and understand a simple text** (learning poverty, World Bank/
  UNESCO). This is not primarily an enrolment problem — net primary enrolment is ~89% — it is a
  *quality* problem. In 2024, **97% of Grade 12 students failed to reach 50%** on national exams.
- The **UHC service coverage index** stands around **39–46%**, and modelling projects only ~65% by
  2030 against an 80% target.

The consequence for a digital strategy is direct: **you cannot assume a literate, numerate user.**
Voice interfaces, iconography, agent intermediation and vernacular language support are not
accessibility features — they are the primary product.

## Why the loop does not self-correct

Standard development logic says: raise agricultural productivity → incomes rise → demand rises →
industry grows → tax base grows → state invests in infrastructure → productivity rises further.

In Ethiopia each arrow is blocked by a specific, identifiable constraint:

| Arrow | What blocks it |
|---|---|
| Productivity → income | Small farm size caps absolute gains; price volatility and middlemen capture the surplus; post-harvest losses destroy 15–25% of the crop |
| Income → demand | Incomes near subsistence; 43% below US$3/day; rural non-farm opportunity limited by land and labour market frictions |
| Demand → industry | Thin, fragmented, low-density demand; poor logistics; FX scarcity for imported machinery; unreliable power |
| Industry → tax base | Large informal sector; weak collection; SOE losses; debt distress |
| Tax base → infrastructure | Debt distress (external debt ~US$28.9 bn in 2024; in default on the 2014 Eurobond); competing reconstruction needs (~US$5 bn in Tigray alone) |
| Infrastructure → productivity | Roads, power, irrigation and connectivity reach rural areas last |

**The digital entry point:** several of these arrows are *information* problems before they are
capital problems. Price discovery, quality verification, identity, credit history, delivery
coordination, subsidy targeting and extension advice are all information-intensive and all
currently handled by slow, expensive, error-prone physical processes. That is where digital
infrastructure has its highest marginal return in Ethiopia — not in replacing capital investment, but
in making the existing capital, labour and produce go further.

## What follows from the diagnosis

1. **Do not start with the hardest problem.** Starting with rural credit reform, land titling, or
   fertiliser supply-chain liberalisation means starting inside the state's slowest processes.
   Start with something a small team can ship: **matching a seller to a buyer and moving money
   safely.**
2. **Design for the median farmer, not the modal startup user.** USSD, voice, agents, vernacular,
   cash-in/cash-out.
3. **Attack information asymmetry, because it is the cheapest thing to fix.** Transparent prices
   alone change bargaining outcomes.
4. **Build the data asset deliberately**, because transaction history is the input to every
   subsequent service (credit, insurance, traceability, subsidy targeting).
5. **Align with the state's own plan (DAR, Digital Ethiopia 2030)** — it lowers political risk and
   opens co-funding, but do not depend on state execution for the product.
