## 1. Macroeconomic position (2026)

Ethiopia enters 2026 as one of Africa's fastest-growing economies and simultaneously one of its most
financially fragile.

| Indicator | Value | Period | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | 9.2% | FY2025/26 | 🟡 |
| Real GDP growth (rebased preliminary) | 7.7% | FY2025/26 | 🟡 |
| IMF projection | 9.2% (2026), 7.8% (2027) | Oct 2026 WEO | 🟢 |
| Fitch projection | ~7% FY27, ~9% FY25 | Oct 2026 | 🟡 |
| Average CPI inflation | 16% (2025) → 11.7% (2026 proj.) | IMF | 🟢 |
| Headline inflation (monthly) | 15.3% | Jul 2026 | 🟡 |
| Exchange rate | ~161 birr/USD | Sep 2026 | 🟡 |
| Nominal GDP (rebased) | ETB 31.86 trn ≈ US$210.7 bn | FY2025/26 | 🟡 |
| External public debt | US$28.9 bn | 2024 | 🟡 |
| Sovereign status | In default on US$1 bn Eurobond; bondholder talks collapsed May 2026 | 2026 | 🟡 |
| IMF programme | US$3.4 bn ECF, 48 months, approved Jul 2024; ~US$2.65 bn disbursed over 5 reviews | 2024–26 | 🟢 |
| Poverty headcount (US$3/day 2021 PPP) | 43% | 2025 proj. | 🟡 |
| FDI | US$4.32 bn | FY2025/26 | 🟡 |

**The reform package.** Since mid-2024 the government has: floated the birr (July 2024), tightened
monetary policy and capped credit, removed fuel subsidies, moved to quarterly electricity tariff
adjustments, opened the telecom sector (Safaricom, 2021 licence; commercial launch Oct 2022), opened
mobile money to foreign-owned firms (2023), established a securities exchange (January 2025), and
begun liberalising the retail and wholesale trade sectors for foreign investment.

**The cost of the reform.** The birr lost roughly two-thirds of its dollar value; inflation peaked
above 26% before easing; poverty rose; and debt restructuring stalled. Growth is real, but it is
growth from a low base with a compressed household purchasing power — which is why **any consumer-
facing digital business must price for a low-ARPU market**, and why B2B and trade facilitation
(basis points on large volumes) is a more robust model than consumer subscriptions.

## 2. The GDP rebasing: what it means and why it matters here

In November 2026 Ethiopia published rebased national accounts using FY2024/25 as the new base year.

| Item | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal GDP FY2024/25 | ETB 15.09 trn | ETB 24.03 trn | +59.2% |
| Nominal GDP FY2025/26 | — | ETB 31.86 trn (~US$210.7 bn) | — |
| Services share | — | 35.91% | — |
| Agriculture share | — | 31.57% | — |
| Industry share | — | 30.20% (up 2.43 pp) | — |
| Crop production | — | ETB 5.07 trn = 21.10% of GDP | +48.65% |

**Implications for this dossier:**
1. Any statistic quoted as "agriculture is ~40% of GDP" is likely pre-rebasing or from an older
   source. The defensible statement is **"about one-third of GDP"** (31.6% rebased; 32.8% World Bank
   for 2025).
2. Debt-to-GDP ratios, tax-to-GDP ratios and sector shares all shift retroactively, which affects
   how a platform's addressable market should be sized (agriculture's absolute value in birr went
   *up*, even as its share went *down*).
3. Time series crossing the break are not comparable. In this dossier, every GDP-share figure states
   its basis.

## 3. The agrarian structure

### 3.1 Smallholder dominance

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Farming households | ~12–15 million |
| Median farm size | <1 ha; many under 0.5 ha |
| Share of cultivated land | ~95% smallholder |
| Share of production | >95% smallholder |
| Commercial farms | <5% of land, concentrated in Gambella, Benishangul-Gumuz, Afar |
| Pastoralist / agro-pastoralist population | ~12–15% (Afar, Somali, Borena, South Omo) |
| Land tenure | All land state-owned (Constitution Art. 40); farmers hold usufruct rights; land cannot be sold, mortgaged or used as collateral |

> ⚠️ **The collateral problem is structural, not technical.** Because land cannot be mortgaged,
> smallholders have almost nothing a formal lender will accept as security. This is the deepest
> reason agricultural credit is 2% of demand. The practical workaround used everywhere — and the one
> this dossier recommends — is **cash-flow-based lending underwritten by verified transaction
> history** (and, where available, warehouse receipts and value-chain guarantees). Digital platforms
> are the cheapest way to create that history.

### 3.2 Productivity

| Indicator | Value | Comparator |
|---|---|---|
| Cereal yield | 2.63 t/ha (2024) | World average ~4.0 t/ha |
| Wheat | ~3.1 t/ha | Potential 5–6 t/ha in highland conditions |
| Maize | ~3.4 t/ha | Regional leaders 4–5 t/ha |
| Teff | ~1.9 t/ha | — |
| Fertiliser use | ~45 kg/ha arable | ~200 kg/ha agronomic benchmark used in Ethiopian studies |
| Mechanisation index | 5.7% | <10% of farmers use any mechanisation |
| Irrigation | 4–5% of cultivated land | — |
| Rain-fed | ~95% | — |

**The yield gap is the opportunity and the trap.** Closing even half the gap between actual and
attainable yields would transform rural incomes, but the gap persists because closing it requires
*simultaneous* access to improved seed, fertiliser, finance, water, advisory and a market that will
pay for the extra output. A single-input intervention (e.g. fertiliser subsidy alone) routinely
fails because the complementary constraints bind.

### 3.3 Livestock

Ethiopia has Africa's largest livestock herd (~60+ million cattle, plus substantial small ruminants
and camels). Livestock contributes a large share of agricultural GDP — commonly cited at ~30–40% of
agricultural value added — and is the main livelihood in the lowlands. It is also the least
digitised, least financed and least formalised part of the sector, and the most exposed to drought.

## 4. Export structure and value capture

| Product | Performance | Structural issue |
|---|---|---|
| **Coffee** | Record US$2.653 bn on 468,967 t (FY2024/25); ~30–35% of export earnings; 4–5 million smallholder growers; ~25% of population dependent on the chain | Farmers capture ~50–61% of FOB — among the lowest of major producers. Only ~19% of coffee is sold as red cherry; 66% of coffee communities lack a wet mill; 58% have no red-cherry buyer |
| **Oilseeds (sesame)** | Major export earner | Raw export; limited processing |
| **Pulses** | Significant | Volatile; low value-add |
| **Horticulture** | Growing | 30–50% post-harvest loss destroys much of the value |
| **Livestock / meat** | Large potential, mostly informal cross-border trade | Sanitary standards, cold chain, certification gaps |
| **Gold** | Overtook coffee in some periods | Artisanal, informal, hard to tax |
| **Textiles / apparel** | Industrial parks; export target missed | Depends on imported inputs (cotton supply chain not integrated) |

**The processing gap.** Analysts repeatedly note that "domestic supply chain ecosystems linking
agriculture to manufacturing have not advanced at the same pace" — Ethiopian factories import raw
materials that Ethiopia itself produces. Cotton for textiles, oilseeds for edible oil, hides for
leather, fruit for juice: the pattern repeats. The cause is not missing factories but **unreliable,
ungraded, unaggregated, un-financed agricultural supply.** A platform that can guarantee volume,
quality and delivery to a processor changes the investment case for the processor.

## 5. The state's role in the economy

| Sector | Structure | Direction of travel |
|---|---|---|
| Telecom | Ethio Telecom (state, 90.12 m customers, 72.09 m active mobile) + Safaricom Ethiopia (15 m 90-day active) | Opened 2021; two-player market |
| Mobile money | telebirr (state) 60.59 m vs M-Pesa 5.2 m active | Opened to foreign-owned firms 2023 |
| Banking | State banks hold roughly half of sector assets; private banks numerous but small | Liberalising gradually; entry of foreign banks pending |
| Payments switch | EthSwitch (bank-owned national switch) | Mandated interoperability, instant payments, QR standard |
| Exchange | Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX) + new securities exchange (Jan 2025) | Digitising |
| Retail / wholesale | Recently opened to foreign investment (40 permits issued) | Early |
| Energy | EEP (generation), EEU (distribution), state-owned | Reform programme with World Bank support |

**What this means for a platform:** the state is simultaneously regulator, competitor (telebirr), and
customer (subsidy and extension programmes). It is not realistic to build a large agri-commerce
platform in Ethiopia **against** the state; the viable posture is to be the best application layer on
state-owned rails, with a public-private structure if scale demands it.

## 6. Structural transformation — where Ethiopia actually is

Development theory expects labour to move from agriculture to industry and services as income rises.
In Ethiopia, agriculture's employment share has fallen only slowly (from ~80% in 1991 to 60.1% in
2025) while its GDP share fell faster (from ~62% in 1992 to ~33% today). The gap between those two
numbers is the productivity problem: **fewer people are producing a smaller share of value, and the
people leaving agriculture are largely moving into low-productivity urban services and informal
trade**, not into manufacturing.

Ethiopia's industrial policy — industrial parks, the Hawassa textile cluster, the GERD-financed
power expansion — has not yet delivered the export-led manufacturing transition that was hoped for.
The realistic near-term growth engine is therefore **agriculture plus agro-processing plus
agri-adjacent services** (logistics, trade, finance), which is precisely the space a platform
occupies.

## Key takeaways

1. Growth is real (9.2%) but household purchasing power is compressed — price for low ARPU.
2. Agriculture is ~one-third of GDP and ~60% of jobs — the only sector large enough to move poverty.
3. Small farm size + no land collateral + thin input use = the loop that must be broken together.
4. Ethiopia exports raw and captures little value; processing is constrained by unreliable,
   ungraded, unaggregated supply — a solvable information and coordination problem.
5. The state is a partner, competitor and customer at once; design accordingly.
6. Use rebased GDP figures and label the basis — the November 2026 rebasing moved everything.
