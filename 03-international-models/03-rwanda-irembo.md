# 03.3 · Rwanda and Irembo

*Part of [03 · International models](README.md)*

> **Why study it.** Rwanda is the closest African analogue to Ethiopia in ambition and constraints
> (small, low-income, post-conflict, agriculture-heavy, PPP-friendly), and Irembo is the clearest
> example of how a **public-private partnership** can deliver citizen services at scale.

---

## 1. What Irembo is

A one-stop e-government portal, launched 2014, built and operated under a **public-private partnership**.
Citizens and businesses apply for and pay for government services through a single online portal or
through a national agent network.

| Metric | Value | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Services available | **240** | 2025 |
| Applications processed | >5 million | 2023–24 |
| Citizen accounts | >400,000 | Mar 2025 |
| Agent network | **3,000+** | 2025 |
| Share of applicants paying digitally | **45%** | 2025 |
| Time saved | **>120 million hours** of travel/queuing | 2025 |
| Reduction in time to obtain services | >80% | 2025 |
| Historical daily user count | ~1,500/day (2020) | 2020 |
| World Bank recognition | Top performer on Doing Business for e-government | historical |

The service catalogue covers civil registration (birth certificates, national ID), driving permits,
business registration, land services, tax declarations, social programmes, and utility payments.

---

## 2. What Irembo teaches that India Stack does not

### 2.1 The agent layer is the actual product

Rwanda's own assessment notes that **more than half of Irembo users prefer to pay an agent in cash**
rather than use the mobile payment system, and that intermediaries emerged spontaneously to fill the
digital-literacy gap. Rwanda formalised this into a 3,000+ agent network.

**Lesson for Ethiopia:** it is not a failure to need agents — it is the design. Budget for it,
professionalise it, and pay for it. The Irembo agent is the direct analogue of the agricultural
cooperative agent proposed in this dossier.

### 2.2 Trust is a legal artefact

Rwanda's ICT Minister explicitly attributes Irembo adoption growth to the **data protection and privacy
law** and to citizens trusting that the system is accountable. The sequence was: legal framework →
trust → adoption. Ethiopia now has the legal framework (Proclamation 1321/2024) — the trust-building
phase is next, and it is earned operationally, not asserted.

### 2.3 PPP beats state-built for delivery

Irembo is operated privately, with government as the service owner. This gives the state political
ownership and the operator product discipline. It also creates a revenue model (service fees) that
does not depend on annual budget appropriations — which is why it has been sustained.

### 2.4 Transparency reduces bribery — measurably

Rwandan user research documents this directly: *"Before Irembo you could bribe a public service
provider to speed things up. Irembo really brought transparency into the public service delivery."*
and *"Irembo decreased corruption and increased transparency."*

**This is the strongest available evidence that digitising a citizen-facing process reduces
discretionary corruption** — a claim that matters in a country scoring 38/100 on the CPI.

### 2.5 Honest limitations

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| Low absolute usage | ~1,500 daily users in 2020; 5 m applications over two years is modest for 14 m people |
| Network reliability | Reported network errors and long loading times |
| Intermediary dependency | Agents became gatekeepers themselves; agent fees are a hidden cost |
| Uneven inclusion | Critics note services do not explicitly address the needs of the poorest, elderly and disabled |
| Cash preference persists | 55% still pay via agent in cash |

**Lesson:** a digital portal does not eliminate intermediation; it **relocates** it. Design the
intermediary into the system (as a paid, accountable agent) rather than pretending it will disappear.

## 3. e-Soko: the agricultural analogue

Rwanda also operates **e-Soko**, a market information system giving farmers commodity prices via
mobile phone — an analogue to Ethiopia's 6077 hotline and part of the same family as 8028.

| Feature | e-Soko | Ethiopia's equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Price information via mobile | Yes | 6077 (National Market Information System) |
| Coverage | Major markets and commodities | ~157 markets, 5 commodities (wheat, maize, teff, sesame, haricot beans) |
| Delivery | SMS, web, radio | IVR/SMS (8028), 6077 |
| Outcome | Improved price transparency; limited evidence of large income effects | Similar — reach is high, measured income effects are modest |

**Honest assessment of market information systems generally:** price information alone produces
**small** welfare gains for smallholders, because the binding constraints are often the ability to
*act* on the information (transport, storage, credit, alternative buyers). This is the strongest
argument for an integrated platform over a price-board-only product.

---

## 4. What Ethiopia should borrow

| Borrow | Do not borrow |
|---|---|
| The PPP delivery model with government as service owner | The assumption that a portal alone drives adoption |
| The formal agent network as a paid, accountable tier | Agent-as-gatekeeper economics (cap agent fees) |
| Legal framework first (data protection → trust → adoption) | The low absolute usage trajectory (~1,500/day after 6 years) |
| Publishing time saved and cost saved as the headline metric | The "digital by default" posture — always keep a paper/agent path |
| The bundling of payments into the service transaction | Fee structures that exclude the poorest (Rwanda charges small fees for most services) |
| Anti-corruption framing — it is a political selling point | — |

## 5. Comparative scorecard

| Dimension | Rwanda | Ethiopia | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Population | ~14 m | ~135.5 m | Ethiopia's scale is 10× — the agent network must be far larger |
| State capacity | High, centralised, disciplined | Lower, federal, uneven | Private operator must supply more of the execution |
| Political continuity | High | Lower | Design for leadership change |
| Digital ID | National ID, mature | Fayda, 53.4 m and scaling | Comparable momentum |
| Payments | Mobile money, mature | Two wallets, interoperable | Comparable |
| Rural connectivity | Better | 22% internet | Ethiopia must be more offline-tolerant |
| Agriculture share of employment | ~60% | ~60% | Identical starting point |
| Land tenure | Registered, titled, digitised | State-owned, untitled | Ethiopia's collateral problem is worse |

## Key takeaways

1. Irembo proves a **PPP e-government model** can work at national scale in a low-income African
   country: 240 services, 3,000+ agents, >120 million hours saved.
2. **Agents are not a workaround — they are the delivery model.** 45% digital payment share after 11
   years is the realistic ceiling, not a failure.
3. **Legal framework → trust → adoption** is the correct sequence; Ethiopia now has the law and must
   earn the trust.
4. Digitising a citizen process measurably **reduces bribery** — the strongest evidence available for
   that claim.
5. Price information systems (e-Soko, 6077) produce modest gains alone: farmers need the ability to
   *act* on prices, which requires transport, storage, credit and alternative buyers — i.e. a platform.
